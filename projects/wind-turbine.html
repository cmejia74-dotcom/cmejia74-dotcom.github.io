A wind turbine that had to lift and light

A turbine built from scratch to do two things at once: raise a weight on a string and light an LED. We started by copying a full-size three-blade turbine. It wouldn’t spin at all, and working out why pushed us toward a completely different kind of rotor.

CEE 34N, Wind Energy ExplainedTeam of three: Josh, Cristian and EshanMy part: airfoil blade design, 3D printing, assemblyBuilt and demonstrated

Two jobs that pull in opposite directions

The brief was to build a working turbine from scratch with two deliverables: raise a cup of coins on a string, and light an LED. Those sound like one problem, but they’re two, and they disagree with each other.

Lifting a mass is a torque problem — you need force at the shaft, and you can afford to turn slowly. Lighting an LED from a generator is an RPM problem — induced voltage depends on how fast the magnetic field changes, so you need speed. A rotor set up for one of those is usually bad at the other.

We started the way most people would: copying the three-blade turbines you see on a ridgeline. That turned out to be the most useful mistake of the project, because the reason it failed is what actually matters for a rotor this small.

Build log

1. Copy a real turbine

I modeled and printed a three-blade airfoil rotor: thin, twisted, tapered blades on a printed spherical hub, scaled down from a full-size machine. On paper that is the right answer, since real turbines use lift-driven airfoils because they are far more efficient.

Put in front of the wind, it would not start turning. Not slowly — at all. A rotor that doesn’t move produces no torque and no voltage, so both deliverables were dead at the same time.

2. It was solidity, not blade shape

The problem wasn’t a bad airfoil — there was barely any blade there. Three thin blades cover very little of the disc the rotor sweeps. That ratio of blade area to swept area is called solidity, and ours was too low to catch enough air to get itself turning.

Full-size turbines can run with low solidity because they’re huge and they sit in strong, steady wind. At desk scale, in gentle indoor air, none of that applies. Watching another team’s high-solidity drag rotor actually work made the fix obvious: stop chasing efficiency and get starting torque from surface area instead.

3. Rebuild around surface area

The second rotor dropped the airfoil entirely. Wide paper-bag sails on dowel spokes give the wind a large, blunt area to push against — drag-driven instead of lift-driven. It is the less elegant machine and the far more effective one: it starts on its own in gentle air.

Everything else hangs off that shaft. The rotor turns about a printed hub at the top of the tower, the generator sits right behind it, and the two deliverables split off the same spin — a string and cup on the mechanical side, the coils and LED on the electrical side.

4. Six magnets on a printed shaft

The generator is hand-built. A 3D-printed PLA shaft carries the magnets and spins inside a box wound with copper wire. As the magnets sweep past, their moving field pushes electrons through the windings and the LED lights.

Instead of the usual four magnets, we used six. More poles means more flux reversals per turn of the shaft, so the current comes more often and more smoothly — which matters when your rotor is a slow-turning drag design and RPM is what you are short on.

One rotor, two outputs

Wind reaches a single shaft, and from there the two outputs are served by completely different physics — one purely mechanical, one electromagnetic. Both are limited by the same thing: how fast that shaft turns.

The drag rotor gives plenty of torque, so the cup comes up without trouble. It turns slowly, though, so the LED never gets driven hard.

What we learned

The useful conclusions all came from the version that didn’t work.

Area decided everything

Whether the rotor turned at all came down to how much of the swept disc was covered, not how refined the blade profile was. Our careful airfoil lost to taped paper bags.

Copying doesn’t transfer

Taking geometry from a machine hundreds of times larger brought along assumptions that didn’t hold here: big blade area and strong, steady wind.

We were short on speed

The drag rotor lifts the weight fine but turns too slowly to drive the LED hard. A gearbox between the shaft and the generator is the obvious next step, trading spare torque for the RPM we lacked.
